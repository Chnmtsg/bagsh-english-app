## How to use this document
This guide compares English and Mongolian system by system. It exists because almost every mistake a Mongolian speaker makes in English is predictable — it comes from a specific structural difference between the two languages, not from carelessness.
Predictable errors are the easy kind to fix. But only if you know where they come from.
Three ways to use it:
| If you are… | Start here |
| Learning English | §KK (the 100-error checklist) → mark your own errors → fix them in blocks of ten |
| Teaching English | Volume 1 §1.3 (the 12 core differences) → then the teaching order in §1.8 |
| Preparing for IELTS | Volume 5 §EE–GG, then the 12-month plan in §LL |


One warning, stated once. Reading this document will not improve your English. Retrieval builds memory; review does not. This is a map. The walking is: read volume, mine vocabulary, get corrected, speak. Use the guide to diagnose — then go and produce.
A note on the Mongolian examples. These are drawn from linguistic sources. If you are a native Mongolian speaker and something here is wrong or oversimplified, correct it. Analysing your own language critically is exactly the skill teaching requires.


# VOLUME 1 — FOUNDATIONS
## 1.1 Why these languages are so far apart
|  | English | Mongolian |
| Family | Indo-European → Germanic → West Germanic | Mongolic |
| Related? | — | Not at all. No shared ancestor. |


English is a cousin of German, Dutch, French, Spanish, Russian, Persian and Hindi. Mongolian is related to none of them.
On "Altaic": you may have seen Mongolian grouped with Turkish, Korean and Japanese in an "Altaic" family. Most historical linguists today reject this grouping. The similarities are real, but they are attributed to centuries of contact and geography (a Sprachbund), not to shared ancestry.

Why this matters practically: a Spanish speaker learning English receives thousands of cognate words and a familiar grammatical shape for free. A Mongolian speaker receives almost nothing for free. Every structure must be built from zero.
This is not an excuse. It is a reason to budget more hours than a European learner would — and a reason not to quit at month eight believing you have failed, when you are simply on a longer curve.

## 1.3 The 12 core differences
Each with: what happens, why, the predicted error, and how to teach it.
### 1 · Word order — the deepest difference
Mongolian is head-final. English is head-initial. This single fact explains at least five of the other problems.
Mongolian: Би ном уншсан. → I · book · read
English: I read a book.

In Mongolian, everything that modifies something comes before it. In English, most modifiers come after. Watch it cascade:
| Structure | 🇲🇳 Mongolian | 🇬🇧 English |
| Verb | object → verb | verb → object |
| Adposition | noun → postposition | preposition → noun |
| Relative clause | the yesterday-drilled → hole | hole → that we drilled yesterday |
| Question | statement + question particle | invert the auxiliary |


Predicted errors: ❌ "I the samples analysed." · ❌ "The yesterday drilled hole was deep." · ❌ "About the report, it's late."
🎓 Teach it as ONE rule, not five: "Mongolian builds backwards; English builds forwards." Draw an arrow →. Students who grasp the single principle stop making all five errors. Students who memorise five rules keep making them.

### 2 · Articles — the biggest single enemy
Mongolian has no articles. English requires one in front of almost every countable singular noun.
But Mongolian DOES mark definiteness — it uses the accusative suffix -ыг / -ийг on specific or definite objects, and often leaves indefinite objects bare. So the concept already exists for your students. They just express it with a case suffix instead of a separate word. Use that as the bridge.
Predicted errors: ❌ "I am geologist." · ❌ "We found new deposit."
Full treatment in §M.
### 3 · Postpositions vs prepositions
| 🇲🇳 Mongolian | Literal | 🇬🇧 English |
| уурхайд | mine-in | in the mine |
| ширээн дээр | table-on | on the table |
| геологиийн тухай | geology-about | about geology |


Mongolian puts the relationship word after the noun. English puts it before.
This is the direct source of the classic error "About English, I understand 60%…" — the postposition тухай has been translated in its Mongolian position.
🎓 Teach it physically. Write the noun on one card and the preposition on another. Have students swap the order with their hands. The motor action fixes it faster than any explanation.

### 4 · No he/she — «тэр» does everything
Mongolian тэр covers he, she, it, and that. English forces a gender choice every single time.
Predicted errors: ❌ "My wife, he works…" · ❌ "My manager, she is a good man."
🎓 This is a SPEED problem, not a knowledge problem. Students know the rule and still fail under time pressure because the choice isn't automatic. Drill: show a photo, describe the person in 3 seconds. Only automaticity fixes it.

### 5 · Verb agreement (-s)
Mongolian verbs change for tense, aspect and mood — but not for person. English adds -s for he/she/it in the present simple.
Predicted errors: ❌ "He work here." · ❌ "The drill produce good samples."
This is the most common error in English learning worldwide.
🎓 The "HE-SHE-IT adds S" chant. It sounds childish. It works. It has to become a reflex, not a decision.

### 6 · Plurals
In Mongolian, plural marking is optional — and after a numeral it's usually dropped, because the number already carries the information.
Mongolian: гурван ном (three book) → English: three books

English marks plural even when it is obvious. English is redundant; Mongolian is efficient.
🎓 Say it plainly: "English repeats itself. This is not logical. Accept it." Students argue with redundancy because their language avoids it. Naming the illogic openly ends the argument.

### 7 · The copula "to be"
Mongolian frequently omits the linking verb.
Mongolian: Би геологич. → I · geologist
English: I am a geologist.

⚠️ That one tiny sentence contains TWO things English forces you to add that Mongolian doesn't have: the verb am and the article a.
🎓 This is the perfect first lesson. One four-word sentence, two of the biggest enemies. Use it on day one.

### 8 · Present perfect
English distinguishes:
| Form | Meaning | Example |
| Past simple | finished, disconnected from now | "I worked there in 2020." |
| Present perfect | started in the past, connected to now | "I have worked here since 2020." |


Mongolian has no direct equivalent. Predicted error: ❌ "I work here since 2020."
🎓 Draw a timeline. Past simple = a dot in the past. Present perfect = an arrow from the past touching today. Then teach the trigger words — since, for, already, yet, just, ever, never — which cover ~80% of correct usage.

### 9 · Relative clauses
Mongolian: [yesterday we drilled] hole — description before
English: hole [that we drilled yesterday] — description after

🎓 Build from two simple sentences, never present the finished form first:
"I saw a hole. We drilled the hole yesterday." → "I saw the hole that we drilled yesterday."

### 10 · Vowel harmony
Mongolian has vowel harmony: vowels within a word must belong to the same group, and suffixes change to match. English has none. English words mix vowels freely and inconsistently.
exploration → /e/ /ə/ /eɪ/ /ə/ — four different vowels
geological → /iː/ /ə/ /ɒ/ /ɪ/ /ə/ — five different vowels

A Mongolian ear may unconsciously try to "smooth" these into agreement.
🎓 Say it directly: "English vowels do not cooperate with each other. Each syllable is independent. Stop looking for a pattern."

### 11 · Stress and rhythm
Covered in full in §1.5. Summary: Mongolian stress is weak, initial, and meaningless. English stress is strong, variable, and changes meaning.
### 12 · Writing systems
|  | Mongolian Cyrillic | English Latin |
| Letter → sound | Nearly 1:1 | Chaotic |
| See a new word | You can read it aloud | You cannot |
| Hear a word | You can spell it | You cannot |


through · though · thought · tough · thorough · plough — six words, -ough spelled identically, six different pronunciations.

🎓 The teaching consequence is absolute: never let a student learn a written word without its audio. In Mongolian, spelling gives the sound for free. In English it does not. No audio = no vocabulary card.

## 1.4 Politeness and evidentiality
### Politeness — why "I want" is a real problem
|  | Mongolian | English |
| Politeness lives in | Pronouns (та / чи) | Verb structures |


Mongolian has та (formal you) and чи (informal you). Change one word, change the entire register.
English has no such pronoun. You is you for your boss, your child, and a stranger. So English moved politeness into grammatical complexity:
The rule: more words + more distance = more polite.

| Directness | Form |
| 😠 Rude | "Give me the report." |
| 😐 Neutral | "I want the report." |
| 🙂 Polite | "I'd like the report, please." |
| 😊 Professional | "Could you send me the report?" |
| 🎩 Very polite | "I was wondering whether you might be able to send me the report." |


⚠️ A Mongolian speaker saying "I want" is not being rude. They are translating та correctly and then losing the politeness, because English doesn't carry it in the pronoun. It must be rebuilt with modal verbs: could, would, might, may.
For business English this may matter more than grammatical accuracy. A grammatically perfect email saying "Send me the data" damages a relationship. A slightly imperfect "Could you possibly send me the data?" does not.
### Evidentiality — the reverse gap
Mongolian marks, in the verb itself, whether you witnessed something or heard about it. English has no grammatical equivalent — it must use separate words.
Mongolian speakers often omit these markers in English, because their brain expects the verb to carry it. The result can sound overconfident:
| Evidence | English |
| I saw it | "The grade is 3 g/t." |
| I read it | "According to the report, the grade is 3 g/t." |
| I heard it | "The grade is reportedly 3 g/t." |
| I infer it | "The grade appears to be around 3 g/t." |


⚠️ This matters enormously in technical writing. Geological reporting depends on distinguishing measured fact from inference. Learn these hedges: appears to · suggests · indicates · is estimated at · according to · based on · is thought to · reportedly.
## 1.5 Pronunciation I — Word stress and rhythm
Word stress is worth more than every consonant and vowel problem combined.

Sounds give you an accent. Accents are fine — millions speak excellent English with one. But wrong stress makes you unintelligible. A native speaker hearing "GE-o-lo-gy" may not recognise the word at all, even if every individual sound was correct.
IELTS examiners score intelligibility, not accent. So stress comes first.
### The core difference
Mongolian: stress is weak, predictable (usually the first syllable), and doesn't change meaning. Every syllable keeps its full vowel.
English: stress is strong, unpredictable, and changes meaning. Worse — unstressed syllables get destroyed. Their vowels collapse into the schwa /ə/.
### The schwa /ə/
/ə/ is the most frequent sound in English. It has no strong quality — it's what you produce when your mouth is completely relaxed: uh.
| Word | A Mongolian speaker says | A native says | What happened |
| geology | ge-o-lo-gy (4 equal) | dʒi-ˈɒl-ə-dʒi | o → ə |
| deposit | de-po-sit (3 equal) | də-ˈpɒz-ɪt | e → ə |
| mineral | mi-ne-ral (3 equal) | ˈmɪn-ə-rəl | two schwas |
| computer | com-pu-ter | kəm-ˈpjuː-tə | two schwas |
| about | a-bout | ə-ˈbaʊt | a → ə |


Stop giving every syllable equal weight. Make ONE syllable strong and crush the rest.
### The stress rules that actually work
English stress is not random — suffixes control it, and the rules are highly reliable.
Rule 1 — -tion / -sion / -cian: stress the syllable immediately BEFORE
exploration → ex-plo-RA-tion · extraction → ex-TRAC-tion · production → pro-DUC-tion · information → in-for-MA-tion · mineralization → min-er-al-i-ZA-tion · precision → pre-CI-sion
Rule 2 — -ity: stress the syllable before
feasibility → fea-si-BIL-i-ty · activity → ac-TIV-i-ty · capacity → ca-PAC-i-ty · density → DEN-si-ty · porosity → po-ROS-i-ty
Rule 3 — -ic / -ical: stress the syllable before
geologic → ge-o-LOG-ic · geological → ge-o-LOG-i-cal · volcanic → vol-CAN-ic · tectonic → tec-TON-ic · statistical → sta-TIS-ti-cal · analytical → an-a-LYT-i-cal
Rule 4 — -ogy / -graphy / -ometry: stress the third syllable from the end
geology → ge-OL-o-gy · lithology → li-THOL-o-gy · mineralogy → min-er-AL-o-gy · technology → tech-NOL-o-gy · geography → ge-OG-ra-phy
⚠️ The trap: GEO-logy ❌ → ge-OL-o-gy ✅. You say your profession's name every day. Get this one perfect first.

Rule 5 — -ate verbs: stress the third syllable from the end
estimate → ES-ti-mate · calculate → CAL-cu-late · evaluate → e-VAL-u-ate · indicate → IN-di-cate · investigate → in-VES-ti-gate
Rule 6 — Neutral suffixes (-ment · -ness · -less · -ful · -er · -ing · -ly · -able): stress does NOT move
de-VEL-op → de-VEL-op-ment · MEA-sure → MEA-sure-ment · a-GREE → a-GREE-ment
Rule 7 — Noun/Verb pairs ⭐ THE MOST USEFUL
Nouns take stress 1. Verbs take stress 2.

| Word | NOUN | VERB |
| survey | SUR-vey (the survey is finished) | sur-VEY (we will survey the area) |
| project | PRO-ject (a mining project) | pro-JECT (project the growth) |
| record | REC-ord (check the records) | re-CORD (record the data) |
| permit | PER-mit (a mining permit) | per-MIT (permit access) |
| contract | CON-tract | con-TRACT (metals contract when cold) |
| extract | EX-tract | ex-TRACT (extract the ore) |
| increase | IN-crease (a 5% increase) | in-CREASE |
| export | EX-port (coal exports) | ex-PORT |
| produce | PRO-duce (agricultural produce) | pro-DUCE (produce a report) |
| transfer | TRANS-fer | trans-FER |


Practise aloud:
"We will sur-VEY the site next week, and the SUR-vey will take three days."
"Please re-CORD the data in the REC-ords."

### Sentence rhythm — and the secret about listening
English is stress-timed. Mongolian is not.
In English, stressed syllables arrive at roughly equal time intervals, and everything between them is squeezed to fit. Read these aloud — they should take the same time:
DOGS eat BONES.
The DOGS will eat the BONES.
The DOGS would have eaten the BONES.

Four beats each. The extra words don't add time — they get crushed.
Weak forms. Function words are almost never pronounced fully:
| Word | Strong | Weak (normal) |
| to | /tuː/ | /tə/ |
| of | /ɒv/ | /əv/ /ə/ |
| and | /ænd/ | /ən/ /n/ |
| for | /fɔː/ | /fə/ |
| can | /kæn/ | /kən/ |
| was | /wɒz/ | /wəz/ |
| are | /ɑː/ | /ə/ |
| have | /hæv/ | /əv/ |
| the | /ðiː/ | /ðə/ |


So "We have to go to the site and look at a lot of samples" actually sounds like:
"We əv tə go tə thə site ən look ət ə lot əv samples."

🔑 THE KEY INSIGHT
If you understand roughly 60–80% of spoken English, the missing 20–40% is mostly this.
It is not unknown vocabulary. It's words you already know perfectly — to, of, have, and, was — hidden inside reductions your ear was never trained on, because textbooks only ever showed you the strong forms.
Your speaking problem and your listening problem are the same problem. When you learn to produce reductions, your ear learns to hear them. Production trains perception. Fix rhythm → speaking improves and listening jumps.

## 1.6 Pronunciation II — Consonants and vowels
### The six predictable consonant enemies
| # | Problem | Why | Fix |
| 1 | Word stress + schwa | Mongolian stresses syllable 1 | Mark stress on every new word. Non-negotiable. |
| 2 | /θ/ /ð/ — "th" | Doesn't exist in Mongolian → becomes s, t, d | Tongue tip between the teeth |
| 3 | /f/ vs /p/ | Mongolian /f/ only in loanwords | fine/pine · fall/Paul · fit/pit |
| 4 | /v/ vs /w/ | Often merged | vest/west · vine/wine · very/wary |
| 5 | English /r/ | Mongolian /r/ is a trill | English /r/ never touches the roof of the mouth |
| 6 | Consonant clusters | Mongolian restricts clusters | Build backwards; insert no vowel |


### 1 · /θ/ and /ð/ — "th"
How: tongue tip between the teeth, blow air. Your tongue must be visible.
/θ/ voiceless: think, three, thirty, thick, depth, north, south, earth, method, lithology, thousand
/ð/ voiced: the, this, that, they, there, then, weather, mother, together, northern
| /θ/ | /s/ or /t/ |
| think | sink |
| thick | sick |
| three | tree |
| thin | sin / tin |
| path | pass |
| mouth | mouse |


Practice sentence: "The thickness of the northern lithology unit is thirty-three meters."

### 2 · /f/ and /v/ vs /p/ and /b/
/f/ /v/: upper teeth touch lower lip, air flows continuously — you can hold fffff forever.
/p/ /b/: lips together, air stops then bursts — cannot be held.
| /f/ | /p/ |
| fine | pine |
| fall | Paul |
| fit | pit |
| fail | pail |
| coffee | copy |


⚠️ "fault" and "fold" are core structural geology terms. If they sound like "palt" and "pold", technical credibility drops instantly.
"The fault runs through the footwall of the folded formation."

### 3 · /v/ vs /w/
/v/ = teeth on lip, buzzing. /w/ = lips rounded like a small kiss; teeth touch nothing.
| /v/ | /w/ |
| vein ⚠️ | wane |
| vest | west |
| vine | wine |
| very | wary |
| verse | worse |


⚠️ "vein" is used every day in geology. "A quartz vein" vs "a quartz wane" — get it wrong and nobody knows what you mean.
"The vein has a high volume of waste within the western wall."

### 4 · English /r/
Mongolian /r/ is a trill or tap — the tongue vibrates against the roof of the mouth.
English /r/ NEVER touches anything.
Pull the tongue back and slightly up, round the lips a little. The tip floats, touching nothing. It should feel strange and lazy. If you hear any tapping, it's wrong.
"The drill rig reached the ore reserve at four hundred meters."

### 5 · /l/ — and the dark L
English has two L sounds:
Clear L at the start of a syllable: level, look, large — tongue tip on the ridge behind the top teeth
Dark L /ɫ/ at the end: drill, coal, gold, metal, model, sample, tunnel, mineral — the back of the tongue rises; it sounds almost like "oo"
Say "drill" — it should end closer to "dri-uhl" than a sharp l.
⚠️ Mongolian л is often produced as a voiceless lateral fricative [ɬ] — air hissing over the sides of the tongue. That sound doesn't exist in English. Make sure your L has voice and no hissing.
### 6 · Consonant clusters
Mongolian restricts which consonants can sit together, so the brain inserts a vowel (epenthesis).
| Word | ❌ Likely error | ✅ Correct |
| strike | si-trike | strike |
| strata | si-trata | strata |
| exploration | ek-si-ploration | exploration |
| extracts | ek-si-tracts | extracts |
| depths | depis / dept | depths /depθs/ |
| drilled | drilled-uh | drilled /drɪld/ |


The fix — build the cluster BACKWARDS:
rike → trike → strike · rata → trata → strata

⚠️ "strike and dip" is the most basic phrase in structural geology. Practise until str has zero vowel inside it.
### The vowels
English has ~20 vowel sounds; Mongolian has roughly 7 short + 7 long.
Good news: Mongolian has phonemic vowel length — a real head start over Japanese or Korean speakers.
Bad news: English pairs differ in quality, not just length.
/iː/ vs /ɪ/ — the most important pair
/iː/ long, tense, lips spread wide · /ɪ/ short, relaxed, mouth more open — not just a short /iː/
| /iː/ | /ɪ/ |
| sheep | ship |
| feel | fill |
| leave | live |
| seat | sit |
| deep | dip ⚠️ |
| reach | rich |


⚠️ "deep" and "dip" are both geology terms with completely different meanings.
/æ/ vs /e/ — /æ/ mouth wide open: sample, sand, black · /e/ half open: set, sediment, level
| /æ/ | /e/ |
| sand | send |
| bad | bed |
| man | men |


/ɜː/ — the "er" sound: work, learn, earth, first, survey, reserve, geothermal. Neutral, unrounded, tongue flat. Does not exist in Mongolian.
/ʌ/ vs /ɑː/ — /ʌ/ short: cut, dust, rough · /ɑː/ long: car, hard, part
## 1.7 Pronunciation training protocol
Pronunciation lives in procedural memory and the motor cortex. It is a physical skill, like a golf swing. Only repetition builds it.
### Six weeks, 15 minutes a day
Weeks 1–2 · Stress and rhythm ⭐ highest priority
5 min: professional words, clapping on the stressed syllable (clapping recruits motor timing)
5 min: noun/verb pairs in full sentences
5 min: weak forms — mark content words, crush everything else
Weeks 3–4 · Problem consonants
5 min: minimal pairs — /θ/, /f-p/, /v-w/. Use a mirror for th — you must see your tongue.
5 min: /r/ and dark /l/ in work vocabulary
5 min: cluster drills, built backwards
Weeks 5–6 · Integration through shadowing
Pick a 60-second clip of natural speech. Same clip all week.
Listen 5×, no speaking
Read the transcript, mark every stressed syllable
Shadow — speak simultaneously, ~0.5s behind
Record yourself; play your version and the original back to back
Repeat until your rhythm matches — not your accent, your rhythm
### Four non-negotiable rules
Mark stress on every new word you learn, forever. Write de-POS-it, never deposit. A word learned without its stress is learned wrong.
Record yourself weekly. You cannot hear your own errors in real time — your brain hears what it intended. Recording is the only honest mirror.
Use a mirror for /θ/, /f/, /v/, /w/. These are visible sounds.
Exaggerate. Aim for 150% of the correct movement; you'll land near 100%. Being polite with your mouth produces a weak accent.
### Free tools
| Tool | Use |
| YouGlish.com | Type any word, hear hundreds of natives say it in real videos. The best free pronunciation tool that exists. |
| Cambridge Dictionary online | IPA + audio, UK and US, every word |
| Rachel's English (YouTube) | The standard channel for connected speech and reductions |
| Your phone's voice recorder | The most important tool on this list |


## 1.8 Teaching English to other Mongolians
### The bilingual teacher's advantage
A native English teacher from London cannot do what a Mongolian teacher can:
| A Mongolian teacher can | A monolingual native speaker cannot |
| Explain why in Mongolian | Only demonstrate, not explain the cause |
| Predict errors before they happen | Only react after |
| Say "I struggled with this too" | Has never experienced it |
| Compare the two systems directly | Doesn't know the L1 system |


For beginners, a good bilingual teacher usually outperforms a monolingual native speaker.
### The honest limit
Teach below your own level. A teacher at IELTS 4.5 can teach A1–A2 students well and should not teach B2+. Teaching above your level fossilises your students' errors, not just your own.
### Teaching accelerates the teacher
The protégé effect is well supported: simply expecting to teach material improves how well you learn it (Nestojko et al., 2014, among others). Preparing to explain forces you to organise knowledge, find gaps, and retrieve actively.
Teaching beginners is one of the fastest routes to B2 yourself.
### What order to teach in
Sequence by frequency × impact, not by textbook order:
| Priority | Topic | Why first |
| 1 | Copula + articles ("I am a geologist") | Appears in every sentence |
| 2 | Word order (SVO) | Fixes five problems at once |
| 3 | Verb agreement (-s) | Highest-frequency single error |
| 4 | Plurals | Constant, easy to fix |
| 5 | Word stress | Biggest listener-facing win |
| 6 | Prepositions vs postpositions | Kills the "about" habit |
| 7 | Present perfect | Later — it's hard |
| 8 | Relative clauses | Later — B1+ only |


### Four teaching mistakes to avoid
Explaining too much. Rules are declarative memory; fluency is procedural. Teach the rule in 2 minutes, drill for 20. Most bad teachers reverse that ratio.
Correcting everything at once. One target per lesson. Correcting every error makes students stop speaking.
Teaching only from books. Every word needs audio — Mongolian spelling is phonetic, English is not.
Praising everything. Students who are always praised stop improving.

# VOLUME 2 — SYSTEMS
## A. The case system
Mongolian marks a noun's role with a suffix. English marks it with a preposition + word position.
| Case | Suffix (approx.) | Job | English equivalent |
| Nominative | — (bare) | subject | word position (before verb) |
| Genitive | -ын / -ийн / -ы / -ий / -н | possession | 's or of |
| Accusative | -ыг / -ийг / -г | definite object | word position (after verb) |
| Dative-Locative | -д / -т | location, recipient | to · at · in · for |
| Ablative | -аас / -ээс / -оос / -өөс | source, comparison | from · than |
| Instrumental | -аар / -ээр / -оор / -өөр | means, tool | with · by |
| Comitative | -тай / -тэй / -той | accompaniment | with |
| Directive | -руу / -рүү / -луу / -лүү | direction toward | to · towards |


### 🔑 Insight 1 — English "with" is less precise than Mongolian
Mongolian uses two different suffixes where English uses one word:
| Meaning | Mongolian | English |
| by means of (a tool) | -аар instrumental | "I cut it with a knife." |
| together with (a companion) | -тай comitative | "I went with my friend." |


When students ask "which 'with' is this?" — the answer is English doesn't care. Naming that openly saves an hour of confusion. It also builds confidence: transfer difficulty runs in both directions.
### 🔑 Insight 2 — Comparison uses the ablative
Mongolian expresses comparison with the ablative case:
Mongolian: [B-from] A big → "Compared-from B, A is big"
English: A is bigger than B

The Mongolian order is reversed and uses a case suffix where English uses than + a comparative form.
Predicted errors: ❌ "This deposit is big from that one." · ❌ "This is more big than that." · ❌ "This is bigger that that one."
🎓 Teach -er + than as one inseparable unit. Never teach -er alone. Then the split:
1 syllable → -er (bigger, deeper, higher) · 3+ syllables → more (more expensive) · 2 syllables → messy, teach as vocabulary

### 🔑 Insight 3 — Why Mongolian speakers omit English prepositions
A Mongolian speaker's brain expects the noun itself to carry the relationship. English puts that information in a separate little word before the noun — a word that, to a Mongolian ear, barely registers.
That's why at, in, on, to, of, for get dropped. They aren't "small unimportant words" — they ARE the case system.
🎓 Teach it this way: "In Mongolian, the ending tells you the role. In English, the little word in front tells you the role. Delete the little word and you've deleted the grammar."

## B. Agglutination
Mongolian stacks suffixes onto one stem. English spreads the same meaning across many separate words.
| Step | Adds | Meaning |
| stem | write | write |
| + causative | make someone do it | make write |
| + passive | done to the subject | be made to write |
| + negative | not | not be made to write |
| + past | completed | was not made to write |


One Mongolian word ≈ eight English words.
### Why this matters more than it looks
A Mongolian speaker has a deep expectation: meaning is dense, and it lives inside the word.
English violates this. English meaning is spread thin across many light words — have, been, to, will, would, of, the, a, is. These are exactly the words that get dropped, because to a Mongolian linguistic instinct they look like noise.
They are not noise. They are the entire grammar.
🎓 Teaching rule: "Mongolian is a train — one engine, many carriages attached. English is a convoy — many small separate vehicles. Lose one vehicle and the cargo is gone."

## C. Topic-prominent vs subject-prominent
The deep explanation for the "about" problem — deeper than postpositions.
|  | Subject-prominent | Topic-prominent |
| Organises around | grammatical subject | the topic |
| Basic shape | SUBJECT + PREDICATE | TOPIC + COMMENT |
| Examples | English, French, Russian | Mongolian, Japanese, Korean, Chinese |


Mongolian leans strongly topic-comment. You announce what you're talking about, then say something about it:
[TOPIC: as for the report] [COMMENT: it's late]

This is completely natural and correct in Mongolian. Hence: ❌ "About the report, it's late." · ❌ "About my English, it's not good."
⚠️ This is not a reasoning error. It is a valid sentence structure that English does not have.

### English is strict: every sentence needs a real subject
English demands a subject so badly it invents fake ones:
| English | Subject | Real? |
| It is raining. | it | ❌ Nothing is raining |
| It is important to study. | it | ❌ Placeholder |
| There are three samples. | there | ❌ Placeholder |
| It takes two hours. | it | ❌ Placeholder |


These dummy subjects exist purely because English cannot tolerate a subjectless sentence.
Predicted errors: ❌ "Is raining." · ❌ "Have three samples." · ❌ "Is important to study English."
### The three English repairs for a topic
| Repair | Example |
| 1. Make the topic the subject ⭐ best | "The report is late." · "My English isn't good." |
| 2. Use a topic phrase | "As for the report, it's late." · "When it comes to my English…" |
| 3. Use a dummy subject | "It's the report that's late." |


🎓 Teach repair #1 first. It's the most natural English and it kills the habit at the root, rather than just swapping about for as for.

## D. Tense and aspect
English separates tense (when) from aspect (how it unfolds). Multiply them → 12 basic forms.
|  | Simple | Continuous | Perfect | Perfect Continuous |
| Past | I worked | I was working | I had worked | I had been working |
| Present | I work | I am working | I have worked | I have been working |
| Future | I will work | I will be working | I will have worked | I will have been working |


Mongolian's verb system is rich too, but organised around completion, duration, and evidence. There is no clean one-to-one mapping.
### Perfect aspect — "a past event that still matters now"
| Form | Meaning | Example |
| Past simple | finished, disconnected | "I worked in Erdenet in 2019." |
| Present perfect | connected to now | "I have worked here since 2019." (still working) |


🎓 Timeline. Past simple = a dot. Present perfect = an arrow from the past touching today.
Trigger words cover ~80% of usage: since · for · already · yet · just · ever · never · so far · recently

### Continuous aspect — "in progress right now"
"I work here." (generally, my job) · "I am working on the resource model." (right now, temporarily)

⚠️ Stative verbs never take continuous: know, understand, believe, want, need, like, own, contain, seem, belong
❌ "I am knowing the answer." → ✅ "I know the answer."
### The reverse gap — evidentiality
Mongolian past-tense forms carry information English cannot express grammatically: did you witness it or hear about it? (Forms such as -лаа / -жээ / -сан carry different evidential and aspectual flavours.)
English has no grammatical evidentiality. It must use separate words — see §1.4.
Learn these: appears to · suggests · indicates · is estimated at · according to · based on · is thought to · reportedly
## E. Questions and negation
### English "do-support" — the strangest thing in the language
Mongolian forms questions by adding a particle (уу / үү / бэ / вэ) at the end. Word order doesn't change.
English inserts a meaningless verb:
| Statement | Question | Negative |
| You work here. | Do you work here? | You don't work here. |
| She works here. | Does she work here? | She doesn't work here. |
| They worked here. | Did they work here? | They didn't work here. |


"Do" means nothing here. It's a grammatical placeholder — English needs an auxiliary to invert or to attach not to, and manufactures one if none exists.
But do-support disappears when a real auxiliary already exists:
|  | Question |
| You are working. | Are you working? ✅ (no "do") |
| You can swim. | Can you swim? ✅ |
| You have finished. | Have you finished? ✅ |
| You work here. | Do you work here? ← only here |


🎓 One rule handles everything: "Find an auxiliary. If there is one, move it to the front. If there isn't one, borrow DO." This covers questions, negatives, short answers and tag questions.

Predicted errors: ❌ "You work here?" · ❌ "Where you work?" · ❌ "Does she works here?" · ❌ "I no have time."
### Negation
⚠️ English forbids double negatives in standard usage:
❌ "I don't have no samples." → ✅ "I don't have any samples."
Teach the pair: some (positive) / any (negative and questions).
## F. Modality
English packs obligation, possibility, permission, advice and politeness into a small set of modal verbs.
| Modal | Core meaning | Example |
| can | ability / informal permission | "I can read the log." |
| could | past ability · polite request · possibility | "Could you send it?" |
| may | formal permission · possibility | "The grade may be higher." |
| might | weaker possibility | "It might rain." |
| must | strong obligation · strong deduction | "You must wear a helmet." |
| should | advice · expectation | "We should re-sample." |
| will | prediction · promise | "I will send it today." |
| would | hypothetical · politeness | "I would like…" |


### Three things that make modals hard
No -s, ever. ❌ "He cans" · ❌ "She musts"
Followed by bare infinitive. ❌ "I can to go" → ✅ "I can go"
Each modal has 2–4 unrelated meanings. Must = obligation and deduction.
### ⭐ Modals are the politeness system
English moved politeness into modal verbs and length because it has no formal/informal pronoun. See the ladder in §1.4.
🎓 Teach it as a ladder, not a list. Students should be able to move a request up and down on demand. For business English this is worth more than perfect tenses.

⭐ The single biggest vocabulary unlock available to a technical English learner.

Mongolian builds new words with native suffixes. English builds its academic and technical vocabulary from Greek and Latin roots — roughly 60%+ of academic English is Latin/Greek derived.
English technical vocabulary is not random. It is assembled from parts.

## H. The Germanic/Latin register split
English was invaded twice and kept both vocabularies. After 1066, French/Latin words entered as the language of court, law, church and administration.
Result: English has two words for almost everything — one Germanic (short, everyday), one Latin/French (long, formal). Almost no other language has such a sharp register split built into its vocabulary.
| 🔨 Germanic — everyday | 🎩 Latin/French — formal |
| get | obtain / acquire |
| start / begin | commence / initiate |
| end / stop | terminate / conclude |
| help | assist / facilitate |
| ask | inquire / request |
| buy | purchase / procure |
| need | require |
| show | demonstrate / indicate |
| find out | determine / ascertain |
| look at | examine / analyse |
| think about | consider / evaluate |
| go up | increase / rise |
| go down | decrease / decline |
| make sure | ensure / verify |
| use | utilise / employ |
| tell | inform / notify |
| deal with | address / handle |
| big | substantial / significant |
| enough | sufficient / adequate |
| about | approximately |


### Why this is a direct IELTS band lever
"We got a lot of samples and looked at them." → Band 5
"We obtained a substantial number of samples and analysed them." → Band 7

Same meaning. Same grammar. Different vocabulary layer.
⚠️ Don't overdo it. Native speakers mix both. Writing that uses only the Latin column sounds artificial and is also penalised. Rule of thumb: Latin words for the technical content, Germanic words for the connective tissue.
🎓 Students find this revelatory. Nobody teaches it, and it explains why their "correct" English still scores Band 5.

## I. Sound structure
### Vowel harmony
Mongolian vowels split into groups, and a word draws from only one:
| Group | Vowels (Khalkha, roughly) |
| Back / "masculine" | а · о · у |
| Front / "feminine" | э · ө · ү |
| Neutral | и |


There is also rounding harmony. This is why one Mongolian suffix has four written forms — -аар / -ээр / -оор / -өөр — all the same suffix wearing different clothes.
English has no harmony whatsoever. See §1.3 #10.
### Phonotactics — syllable structure
English permits enormous consonant clusters — up to 3 consonants before the vowel and 4 after:
| Word | Structure | IPA |
| strengths | CCC-V-CCCC | /streŋkθs/ |
| texts | CV-CCCC | /teksts/ |
| twelfths | CC-V-CCCC | /twelfθs/ |
| extracts | V-CCCC-V-CCC | /ɪkˈstrækts/ |


Mongolian restricts clusters much more, especially word-initially → epenthesis. Fix: build backwards (see §1.6).
## J. Lexical gaps
Every language is detailed about what its speakers care about.
### Where Mongolian is richer
| Domain | Mongolian | English |
| Horses | Hundreds of terms by age, colour, gait, temperament | ~10 (foal, colt, filly, mare, stallion, gelding) |
| Livestock | таван хошуу мал — the "five snouts," each with age/sex/colour terms | generic |
| Kinship | ах (older brother) vs дүү (younger sibling) — age is grammatically required | "brother," "sister" — age unmarked |
| Weather / land | зуд — a specific winter disaster that kills livestock | no single word |
| Culture | гэр, айраг, дээл, хөөмий | borrowed as-is: ger, airag, deel, khoomei |


⚠️ The kinship point causes real errors. Mongolian forces relative age; English has no word for it, so it must be added: "my older brother", "my younger sister". English speakers just say "my brother" — which can feel incomplete to a Mongolian ear. English simply doesn't consider it important.
### Where English is richer
| Domain | Why |
| Science & technology | Absorbed Greek/Latin scientific vocabulary; now imports globally |
| Abstract nouns | Latin supplied thousands: feasibility, sustainability, accountability, methodology |
| Synonyms | The Germanic + French double layer (§H) |
| Business & finance | Global lingua franca of commerce |


🎓 Teaching point: Vocabulary size is not intelligence — it's history and need. English has more mining-finance words; Mongolian has more horse words. Neither language is "better." This matters: students who feel their language is inferior learn worse.

## K. Discourse structure
Invisible to most learners, and it caps thousands of people at Band 6.
|  | English academic style | Many East/Central Asian traditions |
| Main point goes | FIRST | often LAST |
| Structure | conclusion → evidence | context → evidence → conclusion |
| Style | direct, explicit, signposted | contextual, builds gradually |
| Reader's job | easy — told the answer immediately | interpret and infer |


English readers expect the conclusion first. Making them wait reads as evasive or disorganised — even when every sentence is grammatically perfect.
### The rules IELTS rewards
State your position in the introduction. Don't build to it.
Every paragraph starts with a topic sentence.
Signpost explicitly.
One idea per paragraph.
Be direct. Clarity over subtlety.
### Signposting words
| Function | Words |
| Add | furthermore · moreover · in addition · additionally |
| Contrast | however · nevertheless · in contrast · on the other hand |
| Cause | therefore · consequently · as a result · thus |
| Example | for instance · such as · namely · to illustrate |
| Concede | although · despite · while · admittedly |
| Conclude | overall · in conclusion · to sum up |


🎓 Teach structure before grammar for exam students. A well-structured essay with grammar errors often outscores a grammatically clean essay with poor structure — Coherence and Cohesion is a full quarter of the IELTS Writing mark.

## L. Writing systems
Mongolian is unusual — it has been written in multiple scripts, and still is.
| Script | Period | Notes |
| Traditional Mongol bichig | ~1200s–1940s | Vertical, top-to-bottom. Still used in Inner Mongolia; being revived in Mongolia. |
| Cyrillic | 1940s–present | Standard in Mongolia today |
| Latin | brief attempt | short-lived |


English has used the same Latin alphabet for over a thousand years — and that is part of the problem. Pronunciation shifted enormously (especially the Great Vowel Shift, roughly 1400–1700) while spelling froze. English spelling records how words sounded 500 years ago.
knight — the k and gh were once pronounced
through · though · thought · tough · thorough · plough — six sounds, one spelling

|  | Mongolian Cyrillic | English |
| Spelling → sound | Predictable | Unpredictable |
| Hear a word → spell it | ✅ usually | ❌ no |
| See a word → say it | ✅ usually | ❌ no |


🎓 The consequence is absolute: no audio = no vocabulary card. This is a structural requirement created by the difference between the two writing systems, not a preference.

The systems that separate B1 from C1 — and the ones most teachers explain badly.
## M. Articles — the complete system
### The decision tree
1. Is the noun COUNTABLE or UNCOUNTABLE?
      │
      ├─ UNCOUNTABLE ──→ "the" if specific, otherwise NOTHING
      │                   "the water in this pit" / "Water is essential."
      │
      └─ COUNTABLE
              │
              ├─ SINGULAR ──→ ⚠️ MUST have a determiner. Never bare.
              │                    │
              │                    ├─ Listener knows which one? → THE
              │                    └─ New / any one → A / AN
              │
              └─ PLURAL ──→ Listener knows which ones? → THE
                            Otherwise → NOTHING

⚠️ The absolute rule: a singular countable noun can never stand alone. It needs a, an, the, my, this, that, one, each, every… something. ❌ "I am geologist" fails this rule.

### Six triggers for the
| # | Trigger | Example |
| 1 | Second mention | "I found a vein. The vein was 2 m wide." |
| 2 | Only one in this context | "The drill rig broke down." |
| 3 | Defined by what follows | "The hole that we drilled yesterday" · "the top of the hill" |
| 4 | Shared world knowledge | "the government · the weather · the environment" |
| 5 | Superlative / ordinal | "the highest grade · the first sample" |
| 6 | Unique in the universe | "the sun · the moon · the earth · the equator" |


🎓 Trigger #1 is the teaching goldmine. Drill it as a story pattern — a on first mention, the thereafter. That single pattern covers most everyday article use.

### When to use NO article
| Situation | Example |
| Plural, general | "Geologists study rocks." |
| Uncountable, general | "Water is essential." · "Information is valuable." |
| Languages | "He speaks English." ❌ the English |
| Meals | "before lunch" |
| Transport (by + X) | "by car, by plane, on foot" |
| Institutions in their purpose sense | "go to school / work / bed / hospital" |
| Most proper nouns | "Mongolia, Ulaanbaatar, Rio Tinto" |


⚠️ "go to school" (to study) vs "go to the school" (to the building). Zero article = the institution's purpose; the = the physical place.
### 🗺️ Geographical names
| Take THE | Take NOTHING |
| Rivers → the Selenge River, the Orkhon | Lakes → Lake Khövsgöl |
| Deserts → the Gobi Desert | Single mountains → Mount Everest |
| Mountain ranges → the Altai Mountains, the Khangai | Continents → Asia |
| Seas / oceans → the Pacific | Single countries → Mongolia, China |
| Plural countries → the Netherlands, the Philippines | Cities → Ulaanbaatar, Erdenet |
| Countries with Republic/Kingdom/States → the United States | Provinces → Ömnögovi |


Pattern: the attaches to plurals and groups (ranges, island groups, plural nations) and to water and sand (rivers, seas, deserts). Single named peaks and lakes go bare.
### a vs an — sound, not spelling
| a + consonant SOUND | an + vowel SOUND |
| a university /juːnɪ…/ | an hour /aʊə/ (silent h) |
| a European | an honest man |
| a one-metre core /wʌn/ | an MSc /em es siː/ |

### 🎓 Teaching order
The copula sentence: "I am a geologist." One sentence, both missing pieces.
The story pattern: "I saw a rock. The rock was heavy." Repeat with 20 nouns.
Countable vs uncountable (§N) — it controls everything above.
The exceptions. Not before.
Do not start with a rules list. Article rules are declarative knowledge; article use is procedural. Students need hundreds of reps, not a better explanation.
## N. Countable vs uncountable
You can't use articles correctly until you can classify nouns — and English classification is arbitrary.
### ⚠️ The trap list — English uncountables that feel countable
No plural, no "a":
|  |  |  |  |
| information | advice | equipment | machinery |
| furniture | luggage | news | research |
| evidence | knowledge | progress | work |
| money | traffic | weather | software |
| staff | feedback | training | accommodation |

❌ "new equipments" → ✅ "new equipment"
❌ "an advice" → ✅ "a piece of advice"
❌ "The news are good." → ✅ "The news is good."

| Phrase | Use with |

| Uncountable | Countable | Both |

| Business / general | uncountable singular: "the data is ready" |

## O. Gerund vs infinitive
There is no rule — it's lexical memory. Mongolian's verbal noun and converb system doesn't split this way, so nothing transfers.
### 🔵 Verb + -ing
enjoy · avoid · finish · mind · suggest · consider · practise · recommend · admit · deny · risk · involve · keep · imagine · miss · postpone · delay · give up
"We considered drilling deeper." · "I enjoy working in the field."

### 🟢 Verb + to
want · need · decide · hope · plan · agree · promise · refuse · offer · manage · expect · learn · seem · afford · fail · arrange · intend · attempt
"We decided to drill deeper." · "I hope to finish by Friday."

### 🟡 Both, same meaning
like · love · hate · prefer · begin · start · continue
### 🔴 Both, DIFFERENT meaning ⚠️
| Verb | + -ing | + to |
| remember | "I remember locking it." — memory of a past act | "I remembered to lock it." — didn't forget the task |
| forget | "I'll never forget seeing it." | "I forgot to send it." |
| stop | "He stopped smoking." — quit | "He stopped to smoke." — paused in order to |
| try | "Try restarting it." — experiment | "Try to restart it." — make an effort |
| regret | "I regret telling you." — sorry I did | "I regret to inform you…" — sorry I must now |
| mean | "It means working late." — involves | "I meant to call." — intended |


### ⭐ The one reliable rule: after a preposition → always -ing
"interested in learning" · "good at drilling" · "before starting" · "instead of waiting" · "capable of producing"

⚠️ The trap that costs professionals credibility:
✅ "I look forward to hearing from you."
❌ "I look forward to hear from you."

Here to is a preposition, not part of an infinitive. Same with:
"I'm used to working nights."
"We're committed to reducing emissions."
"This is due to increasing costs."
"In addition to drilling, we surveyed."
Learn "I look forward to hearing from you" as a single fixed block and never analyse it.
## P. Phrasal verbs
### Why they're structurally alien to a Mongolian speaker
|  | Mongolian | English phrasal verb |
| Verb meaning modified by | suffixes, attached, fixed | a separate floating word |
| Position | always attached to the stem | can move |
| Meaning | compositional, predictable | non-compositional |


Give up has nothing to do with give or up. Mongolian builds meaning inside the word; English places a small word beside the verb — and that small word can jump.
### The four types
1. Intransitive — no object
break down · show up · get up · take off · come back

2. Transitive separable — object before or after the particle
turn on/off · pick up · write down · fill in · look up · set up · put off
"Turn on the light" ✅ · "Turn the light on" ✅

⚠️ A pronoun MUST go in the middle: "Turn it on" ✅ · "Turn on it" ❌

3. Transitive inseparable — never split
look after · deal with · look into · run into · get over · come across
"Look after the samples" ✅ · "Look the samples after" ❌

4. Three-part — never split
put up with · look forward to · come up with · get away with · run out of

### One verb, many lives — "get"
|  | Meaning |
| get up | rise from bed |
| get on | board / progress |
| get off | leave a vehicle |
| get over | recover from |

| get through | finish / make contact |

| get into | enter / become interested |
| get out of | avoid a duty |

## Q. Collocation
Collocation = which words habitually go together. Not grammar, not vocabulary — the third thing, and what makes English sound native or foreign.
❌ "We did a decision." — grammatically perfect, completely wrong
✅ "We made a decision."

No grammar book explains why. It just is.
### The five types
| Type | Examples |
| verb + noun | make a decision · take a sample · conduct a survey · reach a conclusion · meet a deadline |
| adjective + noun | heavy rain · strong wind · high grade · deep concern · significant increase |
| adverb + adjective | highly significant · deeply concerned · widely used · fully aware |
| verb + preposition | depend on · result in · consist of · comply with · account for |
| noun + noun | drill core · resource estimate · feasibility study · risk assessment |


### MAKE vs DO
| MAKE (create something new) | DO (perform an activity) |
| make a decision | do research |
| make a mistake | do work |
| make progress | do business |
| make an effort | do an experiment |
| make a suggestion | do a favour |
| make an arrangement | do damage |
| make a profit | do the analysis |
| make a plan | do your best |


### ⛏️ Professional collocations — learn as complete blocks
|  |  |
| conduct a survey | carry out an analysis |
| collect / take samples | drill a hole |
| estimate resources | delineate a deposit |
| assess the risk | interpret the data |
| log the core | assay the samples |
| define an anomaly | model the orebody |
| high-grade ore | low-grade material |
| proven and probable reserves | measured, indicated and inferred resources |
| due diligence | feasibility study |
| cut-off grade | strip ratio |


⚠️ measured · indicated · inferred · proven · probable are legally defined terms in the JORC Code and NI 43-101. In technical reporting they are not interchangeable synonyms. See §CC.

### 🎓 How to learn collocations
Record phrases, not words. Your card says "conduct a survey", never "conduct".
Oxford Collocations Dictionary (free online) — type a noun, see every verb and adjective natives pair with it.
YouGlish for real pairings in context.
When you meet a new noun, ask: what verb goes with it?
This is why vocabulary must be learned in sentence cards, not word pairs. A word learned alone is a word you will collocate wrongly forever.

## S. Conditionals
### The five patterns
| Type | Structure | Use | Example |
| Zero | If + present, present | general truth, science | "If you heat limestone, it decomposes." |
| First | If + present, will | real future | "If we drill deeper, we will find higher grades." |
| Second | If + past, would | unreal now | "If I had more time, I would study more." |
| Third | If + past perfect, would have | unreal past | "If we had drilled there, we would have found it." |
| Mixed | If + past perfect, would | past cause → present result | "If we had drilled there, we would be rich now." |


### 🔑 The insight that makes it click
English uses the PAST TENSE to mark UNREALITY, not past time.

"If I had more time…" — had is past tense, but the meaning is right now. It's past-shaped because it's not true.

The rule: step the tense one back to signal "this is not real."
real present → past form · real past → past perfect form
🎓 Teach it as "distance": the further the grammar moves into the past, the further the meaning moves from reality.

### Two absolute rules
1. Never if + will in the condition clause.
❌ "If it will rain, we stop." → ✅ "If it rains, we will stop."

2. Use were for all persons in the second conditional (the surviving English subjunctive):
"If I were you…" · "If the grade were higher, the project would be viable."

### Alternatives to "if"
| Word | Meaning | Example |
| unless | if not | "Unless it rains, we'll drill." |
| provided that | only if | "Provided that funding arrives…" |
| as long as | on condition | "As long as grades hold…" |
| in case | as a precaution | "Bring spares in case it breaks." |
| otherwise | if not, then | "Fund it, otherwise we stop." |


### ⭐ Inversion — a Band 8 feature
| Normal | Inverted (formal) |
| If you should need help… | Should you need help… |
| If I were to resign… | Were I to resign… |
| If we had known… | Had we known… |


"Had we known the grade was this low, we would have halted the programme."

Two or three per essay signal genuine range. Use sparingly.
## T. Word order inside the sentence
### ⭐ Adjective order — the rule nobody knows they know
Opinion → Size → Age → Shape → Colour → Origin → Material → Purpose → NOUN

"a beautiful large old round black Mongolian wooden storage box"

Change the order and it sounds broken to a native ear — "a wooden black old large box" — even though no rule was ever taught. Native speakers cannot explain this. They just hear it.
In practice, rarely more than three:
"a large old drilling rig" · "a high-grade copper deposit" · "a thick grey sedimentary unit"

### Adverb placement
Frequency adverbs (always, usually, often, sometimes, rarely, never):
BEFORE the main verb: "I always check the logs."
AFTER the verb be: "I am always careful."
AFTER the first auxiliary: "I have always worked here."
⚠️ Never place an adverb between a verb and its object:
❌ "He examined carefully the core."
✅ "He examined the core carefully." · "He carefully examined the core."

### SVOMPT
Subject → Verb → Object → Manner → Place → Time
"We | drilled | three holes | carefully | at the site | last week."

Time may move to the front for emphasis — "Last week we drilled three holes" ✅ — but never to the middle:
❌ "We drilled last week three holes."

🎓 Mongolian's freer word order (case suffixes mark roles, so position matters less) means students will scramble English constituents without feeling any error. Give them SVOMPT as a physical card-sorting exercise.

## U. Idiom logic
Idioms look random. They aren't. English organises abstract thought through a small number of conceptual metaphors (Lakoff & Johnson).
### The master metaphors

🎓 Teach the metaphor family, not individual idioms. Students who grasp TIME IS MONEY will correctly produce spend, save and waste with time, and will guess invest time without being taught it.

# VOLUME 4 — STRUCTURE, REGISTER, PROFESSION
## V. Relative clauses
Mongolian: [that-we-drilled-yesterday] hole — description before
English: hole [that we drilled yesterday] — description after

Mongolian uses participles before the noun and has no relative pronouns at all. Nothing transfers — build from zero.
### The relative pronouns
| Pronoun | Refers to | Example |
| who | people (subject) | "the geologist who logged the core" |
| whom | people (object, formal) | "the engineer whom we hired" |
| which | things | "the deposit which we found" |
| that | people or things (defining only) | "the hole that we drilled" |
| whose | possession — people AND things | "a deposit whose grade exceeds 2 g/t" |
| where | place | "the site where we camped" |
| when | time | "the year when production started" |


⚠️ "whose" is not only for people. "a fault whose orientation is unclear" is correct.
### ⭐ Defining vs non-defining — the comma changes the meaning
|  | Defining (restrictive) | Non-defining (extra info) |
| Commas | ❌ none | ✅ required |
| Can use that | ✅ yes | ❌ never |
| Can omit pronoun | ✅ sometimes | ❌ never |
| Function | tells you which one | adds a side comment |


"The geologists who worked overtime got a bonus."
→ Only some worked overtime. Only they got a bonus.

"The geologists, who worked overtime, got a bonus."
→ All of them worked overtime. All got a bonus.

Two punctuation marks. Completely different facts. In a technical or legal document this matters enormously.
### When you can delete the relative pronoun
Only when it is the object:
|  |  |
| ✅ "the samples (that) we collected" | that = object → can delete |
| ❌ "the samples that arrived yesterday" | that = subject → cannot delete |


Test: is there already another subject after the pronoun? If yes, you may delete it.
### ⭐ Reduced relative clauses — the key to technical writing
| Full | Reduced |
| "the samples that were collected yesterday" | "the samples collected yesterday" |
| "the unit which overlies the granite" | "the unit overlying the granite" |
| "mineralisation that is hosted in shear zones" | "mineralisation hosted in shear zones" |
| "a dyke which intrudes the sequence" | "a dyke intruding the sequence" |


Rule: -ing = active meaning (overlying, intruding) · -ed / past participle = passive meaning (hosted, overlain, intruded)
This is why professional geological English is full of hosted in · overlain by · intruded by · bounded by · truncated by. They are all reduced relative clauses. Master this and your technical writing immediately sounds professional.

### Prepositions in relative clauses
| Register | Form |
| Informal / spoken | "the hole that we drilled in" |
| Formal / written ⭐ | "the hole in which we drilled" |


⚠️ Never "the hole in that" — that can never follow a preposition.
Formal patterns: in which · of which · from which · to which · by which · at which
"the interval from which the sample was taken"

## W. The passive voice
Form: be + past participle
Mongolian does have a passive suffix (-гд-), so the concept exists. What differs is how often and why English uses it — and English's impersonal passive has no Mongolian equivalent.
### The six reasons English chooses passive
| # | Reason | Example |
| 1 | Agent unknown | "The samples were contaminated." |
| 2 | Agent obvious | "He was arrested." |
| 3 | Agent unimportant | "Twelve holes were drilled." |
| 4 | ⭐ Objectivity — science & technical writing | "The core was logged and sampled." |
| 5 | Information flow — known first, new last | "The anomaly was identified by a soil survey." |
| 6 | Diplomacy — avoid naming blame | "Mistakes were made." |


Reason 4 is why the mining industry writes in passive. In a technical report, what happened matters; who did it usually doesn't.
### All the forms
| Tense | Active | Passive |
| Present simple | we log the core | the core is logged |
| Past simple | we logged the core | the core was logged |
| Present perfect | we have logged it | it has been logged |
| Past perfect | we had logged it | it had been logged |
| Future | we will log it | it will be logged |
| Modal | we must log it | it must be logged |
| Continuous | we are logging it | it is being logged |
| Infinitive | to log it | to be logged |


### ⭐ The impersonal passive — pure academic English
No Mongolian equivalent. Memorise as fixed blocks:
"It is believed that the mineralisation is structurally controlled."
"It is estimated that the deposit contains 2 Mt."
"The intrusion is thought to be Devonian in age."
"The unit is interpreted as a debris flow."
"Grades are reported to be in the range of 1–3 g/t."

⚠️ This is the evidentiality system (§1.4, §D) in English clothing. Skip these and you sound like you're asserting inference as fact.
### Common errors
| ❌ | ✅ | Why |
| "The accident was happened." | "The accident happened." | Intransitive verbs have no passive |
| "It was consisted of three units." | "It consisted of three units." | Same |
| "The report was wrote." | "The report was written." | Wrong participle |
| "The samples was analysed." | "The samples were analysed." | Agreement |


### 📌 A note on modern style
Many scientific journals now prefer active voice with "we". But mining technical reports (JORC, NI 43-101) remain firmly passive and impersonal. Match the voice to the document type.
## X. Reported speech
### The backshift rule
| Direct | Reported |
| present simple → | past simple |
| present continuous → | past continuous |
| past simple → | past perfect |
| present perfect → | past perfect |
| will → | would |
| can → | could |
| may → | might |
| must → | had to |


"I am tired." → He said he was tired.
"We will finish on Friday." → She said they would finish on Friday.

### ⚠️ When NOT to backshift
Reporting verb is present: "He says he is tired."
Still true / general truth: "She said water boils at 100 °C."
Recent and still relevant: "He said he is on his way."
### Time and place shifts
| Direct | Reported |
| now | then |
| today | that day |
| tomorrow | the next / following day |
| yesterday | the previous day |
| here | there |
| this | that |
| ago | before |


### ⚠️ Reported questions — the biggest error source
NO inversion · NO do-support · NO question mark

| Direct | ❌ Wrong | ✅ Correct |
| "Where do you work?" | He asked where did I work? | He asked where I worked. |
| "What is the grade?" | He asked what was the grade? | He asked what the grade was. |
| "Do you work here?" | He asked do I work there. | He asked if / whether I worked there. |


The rule: once reported, a question becomes a statement. Statement word order. Full stop. Yes/no questions need if or whether.
### Reported commands
tell / ask + person + to-infinitive
"Send the report." → He told me to send the report.
"Don't touch it." → He told me not to touch it.

### ⚠️ say vs tell
| Verb | Pattern |
| say | say something · say to someone |
| tell | tell someone something ← needs a person |


❌ "He said me the results." → ✅ "He told me the results."
❌ "He told that it was ready." → ✅ "He said that it was ready."

### ⭐ Reporting verbs beyond say/tell
| Neutral | Positive | Negative | Strong |
| state · note · observe · report · mention · point out | suggest · recommend · propose · advise | deny · complain · warn · dispute | insist · emphasise · argue · claim · conclude |


⚠️ Watch the pattern each demands:
| Verb | Pattern |
| suggest / recommend | + -ing, or + that clause — ❌ never "suggest me to do" |
| advise / warn / remind | + person + to |
| admit / deny | + -ing |
| insist / propose | + that + base form |


✅ "He suggested drilling deeper." / "He suggested that we drill deeper."
❌ "He suggested us to drill deeper."

## Y. Determiners and quantifiers
### The determiner slots
PRE-determiner → CENTRAL determiner → POST-determiner → adjectives → NOUN
"all | the | three | large | samples"

⚠️ Only ONE central determiner:
❌ "the my report" → ✅ "my report"
❌ "a my friend" → ✅ "a friend of mine"

A friend of mine, a colleague of ours — learn as a fixed pattern.
### The key distinctions
| Pair | Difference |
| each vs every | each = one by one · every = all as a group. Both take a singular verb. |
| both / either / neither vs all / any / none | first set = exactly two · second = three or more |
| some vs any | some = positive statements + offers/requests · any = negatives, questions |
| few vs a few | few = almost none 😟 · a few = some 🙂 |
| little vs a little | little = almost none 😟 · a little = some 🙂 |
| all samples vs all of the samples | general vs these specific ones |


### another / other / the other / others
| Form | Meaning | Example |
| another | one more, singular | "Let's drill another hole." |
| other + plural | additional ones | "other methods" |
| the other | the remaining one | "the other side of the fault" |
| the others | the remaining ones | "The rest arrived; the others are late." |


⚠️ another is one word and always singular. ❌ "another samples"
### its vs it's
| Form | Meaning |
| its | possessive — "the deposit and its grade" |
| it's | it is / it has — "It's ready." |


The trick: if you can replace it with "it is", use it's. Otherwise its.
Native speakers get this wrong constantly. A learner who learned it as a rule has the advantage.
## Z. Punctuation
### ⚠️ The comma splice — the signature Mongolian-speaker error
❌ "Let's forget about teaching me, I want to explore more."

A comma cannot join two complete sentences. Ever. Four legal repairs:
| Repair | Example |
| 1. Full stop | "…teaching me. I'd like to explore more." |
| 2. Semicolon | "…teaching me; I'd like to explore more." |
| 3. Comma + conjunction | "…teaching me, so I'd like to explore more." |
| 4. Subordinate it | "Instead of teaching me, explore the differences." |


Test: cover the comma. Are both halves complete sentences alone? If yes, a comma is illegal.
### When commas ARE required
| # | Rule | Example |
| 1 | Lists | "core, chips, and pulps" |
| 2 | Before FANBOYS joining two clauses | "We drilled, but we found nothing." |
| 3 | After an introductory element | "In 2019, production began." · "However, costs rose." |
| 4 | Around non-defining clauses | "Hole DDH-01, which we drilled first, was barren." |
| 5 | Around appositives | "Batbold, the site geologist, logged the core." |


FANBOYS = for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so.
### Where commas are FORBIDDEN
❌ Between subject and verb: "The samples we collected, were analysed."
❌ Before a defining relative clause: "the hole, that we drilled"
❌ After a conjunction: "but, we found nothing"
### The other marks
| Mark | Job | Example |
| ; | joins two closely-related complete sentences | "Grades were low; the project was shelved." |
| : | introduces a list or explanation | "Three units were logged: shale, siltstone and tuff." |
| — | emphasis or aside (informal) | "The grade — surprisingly — exceeded 3 g/t." |
| ' | possession + contraction | "the geologist's report" |


### Apostrophes
| Form | Use |
| geologist's report | one geologist |
| geologists' reports | many geologists |
| ❌ sample's | never for plurals |
| it's | it is / it has |
| its | possessive |


### ⭐ Hyphens in compound modifiers
Hyphenate a compound adjective when it comes BEFORE the noun:
| Before the noun (hyphen ✅) | After the noun (no hyphen) |
| a high-grade deposit | the deposit is high grade |
| a 500-metre hole | the hole is 500 metres deep |
| a well-known method | the method is well known |
| open-pit mining | mining in an open pit |


⚠️ "a 500-metre hole" — singular metre inside a compound modifier.
### Capitalisation
| Always capital | Never capital |
| I | seasons (summer, winter) |
| languages, nationalities (English, Mongolian) | directions as directions (drive north) |
| days, months (Monday, July) | general nouns (geology, mining) |
| proper nouns (Ulaanbaatar, Oyu Tolgoi) | job titles in general use (a geologist) |
| formal geological unit names (the Khanbogd Complex) | informal rock names (granite, shale) |


### ⚠️ Decimal separators — a real professional risk
English-language international reporting uses:
period for decimals → 3.5 g/t
comma for thousands → 1,250,000 tonnes
Several conventions elsewhere (including the Russian-influenced tradition) reverse this — 3,5. Verify which convention you're used to. In a resource statement, 1,250 meaning 1.25 versus 1250 is a three-order-of-magnitude error.
## AA. Academic register & hedging
### The eight features
| # | Feature | ❌ Informal | ✅ Academic |
| 1 | Formal vocabulary | "we got results" | "results were obtained" |
| 2 | No contractions | "don't, it's, we've" | "do not, it is, we have" |
| 3 | No phrasal verbs | "find out, look into" | "determine, investigate" |
| 4 | Impersonal | "I think the grade is high" | "the grade appears to be high" |
| 5 | Hedged | "this proves" | "this suggests" |
| 6 | Nominalised | "we drilled and it cost less" | "drilling resulted in a cost reduction" |
| 7 | Precise | "a lot of samples" | "347 samples" |
| 8 | No idiom | "the tip of the iceberg" | "an indication of a broader issue" |


### ⭐ Hedging — the skill that separates Band 6 from Band 8
Hedging = softening a claim so you don't assert more than your evidence supports. Academic and technical English is obsessed with it. Overclaiming is treated as worse than a grammar error.
#### The strength ladder
| Strength | Form |
| 💪💪💪 | "X causes Y." |
| 💪💪 | "X leads to Y." |
| 💪 | "X is likely to cause Y." |
| 🤏 | "X may cause Y." |
| 🤏🤏 | "X appears to cause Y." |
| 🤏🤏🤏 | "Evidence suggests that X may contribute to Y." |


#### The hedging toolkit
| Type | Words |
| Modals | may · might · could · would |
| Verbs | appear · seem · suggest · indicate · tend to · is thought to |
| Adverbs | possibly · probably · apparently · arguably · relatively · generally |
| Adjectives | likely · possible · probable · apparent |
| Nouns | possibility · likelihood · tendency · assumption |
| Distancing | it is thought that · according to · evidence suggests · based on |


#### Boosters — the opposite, use sparingly
clearly · undoubtedly · certainly · demonstrate · establish · confirm
⚠️ In geological reporting, an unearned "clearly demonstrates" is a credibility problem, not a style problem.
### Formal replacements
| ❌ Avoid | ✅ Use |
| a lot of / lots of | a significant number of · numerous |
| big / huge | substantial · considerable |
| get | obtain · receive · acquire |
| thing / stuff | factor · element · aspect |
| good / bad | favourable / adverse · beneficial / detrimental |
| show | demonstrate · indicate · reveal |
| look at | examine · analyse · assess |
| find out | determine · establish |
| a bit | slightly · marginally |
| etc. / and so on | (delete — list properly) |
| you | one · researchers · the reader |
| Also, … | Furthermore, · In addition, · Moreover, |
| But | However, · Nevertheless, |
| So | Therefore, · Consequently, |


⚠️ Never begin an academic sentence with And, But, So, Also.

### 📊 Task 1 (Academic) — 150 words, 20 minutes
Describe a graph, chart, table, process or map. No opinion. No conclusion.
| Part | Content |
| 1. Introduction | Paraphrase the question in one sentence |
| 2. ⭐ Overview | 2 key features. No numbers. |
| 3. Body 1 | Detail with specific data |
| 4. Body 2 | Detail with specific data |


⚠️ The Overview is the single most important paragraph. Examiners look for it explicitly. No overview = capped at Band 5 on Task Achievement, regardless of everything else. Signal it: "Overall, …"

#### Trend vocabulary
| Direction | Verbs | Nouns |
| ⬆️ Up | rise · increase · grow · climb · surge · soar | a rise · an increase · a surge |
| ⬇️ Down | fall · decrease · decline · drop · plummet · plunge | a fall · a decline · a drop |
| ➡️ Flat | remain stable · level off · plateau · stabilise | a plateau |
| 〰️ Variable | fluctuate · vary | a fluctuation |

Adverbs: sharply · dramatically · significantly · steadily · gradually · slightly · marginally

"Costs rose sharply." (verb + adverb) · "There was a sharp rise in costs." (adjective + noun)

### 📝 Task 2 — 250 words, 40 minutes ⭐ Worth TWICE Task 1
#### The five question types
| Type | What it asks |
| Opinion | "To what extent do you agree or disagree?" |
| Discussion | "Discuss both views and give your opinion." |
| Problem/Solution | "What problems does this cause and how can they be solved?" |
| Advantages/Disadvantages | "Do the advantages outweigh the disadvantages?" |
| Two-part | Two direct questions |


⚠️ Answer the question that was actually asked. The most common Band 5 cause is writing about the topic instead of the question.
#### The structure
| Paragraph | Content |
| 1. Introduction | Paraphrase the topic + state your position clearly |
| 2. Body 1 | Topic sentence → explain → example |
| 3. Body 2 | Topic sentence → explain → example |
| 4. Conclusion | Restate your position. No new ideas. |


⭐ State your position in the introduction. Building slowly toward a conclusion is normal in many rhetorical traditions and reads as evasive to an examiner (§K).

#### The marking criteria — 25% each
| Criterion | Measures |
| Task Response | Did you answer the full question with a clear position? |
| Coherence & Cohesion | Structure, paragraphing, linking |
| Lexical Resource | Range and precision of vocabulary |
| Grammatical Range & Accuracy | Variety of structures + error rate |


Structure is worth as much as grammar.
#### Roughly what Band 7 requires
Addresses all parts, with a clear consistent position
Logical organisation, clear progression, effective paragraphing
Some less common vocabulary used with awareness of collocation
A mix of complex structures; the majority of sentences error-free
#### The seven ways people lose marks
No clear position
No topic sentences
Memorised template phrases — examiners are trained to spot and penalise these
Under 250 words — automatic penalty
Answering the topic, not the question
Only simple sentences
Poor time management — do Task 2 first if you're slow; it's worth double
## CC. Technical report English — JORC & NI 43-101
### The reporting codes
| Code | Region |
| JORC Code | Australasia |
| NI 43-101 | Canada |
| SAMREC | South Africa |
| PERC | Europe |
| S-K 1300 | United States |
| CRIRSCO Template | the international standard these align to |


Mongolian projects are typically reported under JORC or NI 43-101 for foreign listings.
### ⚠️ The terminology is legally defined — these are NOT synonyms
Exploration Results
        ↓
Mineral Resources    →  Inferred → Indicated → Measured
        ↓                       (increasing geological confidence)
Ore Reserves         →  Probable ← Indicated
                        Proved   ← Measured

| Term | Meaning |
| Mineral Resource | Material with reasonable prospects for eventual economic extraction |
| Ore Reserve | The economically mineable part, after applying Modifying Factors |
| Modifying Factors | mining · processing · metallurgical · infrastructure · economic · marketing · legal · environmental · social · governmental |
| Competent Person (JORC) / Qualified Person (NI 43-101) | A legally defined, named, accountable individual |


⚠️ A Resource is not a Reserve. Using them interchangeably in a public document is a regulatory offence, not a language slip.

⚠️ Do not call unmined material "ore." Ore implies demonstrated economic viability. The correct neutral terms are mineralisation or mineralised material. This rule is broken constantly by non-native and native speakers.

### Standard phrasing to know by heart
"The Mineral Resource estimate was prepared in accordance with the JORC Code (2012 Edition)."
"Mineral Resources are reported at a cut-off grade of 0.3 % Cu."
"No Ore Reserves have been declared for the Project."
"Results are reported as downhole intervals; true widths are estimated to be approximately 70 % of downhole widths."
"Assays were performed by ALS Global, an accredited laboratory independent of the Company."
"QAQC procedures included the insertion of certified reference materials, blanks and duplicates at a rate of 1 in 20."
"The Competent Person for this statement is Mr X, a Member of the AusIMM, who has sufficient experience relevant to the style of mineralisation under consideration."

### The verbs of geological description
| Verb | Use |
| comprises / consists of | composition — ⚠️ "is comprised of" is disputed; prefer comprises |
| is hosted by / in | where mineralisation sits |
| is overlain / underlain by | stratigraphic position |
| is intruded by | crosscutting igneous body |
| is truncated by / offset by | faulting |
| strikes / dips / plunges | orientation |
| is interpreted as / to be | ⚠️ hedged — an interpretation, not a fact |
| was intersected | drilling encountered it |
| returned | assay results |
| grades (verb) | "the interval grades 1.8 g/t Au" |


### The intercept format
"Hole KHB-024 returned 25 m @ 1.8 g/t Au from 120 m."

Spoken: "Hole K-H-B twenty-four returned twenty-five metres at one point eight grams per tonne gold from one hundred and twenty metres."
⚠️ @ is spoken as "at"; g/t as "grams per tonne." Practise saying intervals aloud.
### Register summary
| Feature | Convention |
| Voice | Passive, impersonal |
| Person | Never "I" — use "the Company", "the author", "the Competent Person" |
| Hedging | Mandatory — measured, indicated and inferred must be linguistically distinguished |
| Tense | Past for work done; present for current interpretation |
| Units | Always explicit — g/t, %, ppm, Mt, m, koz |
| Contractions | Never |

### 🎯 The training protocol — dictation
15 minutes a day:
Take 60 seconds of natural native speech — a podcast or interview, not a graded lesson
Write down exactly what you hear. Struggle. Replay as needed.
Compare against the transcript
⭐ Circle every word you already knew but failed to hear
Those circles are your real listening problem — study which reduction hid the word
Shadow the clip until you can produce it at full speed with the reductions
Producing the reductions is what trains your ear to hear them. Perception and production share the same neural machinery.

Expect a measurable jump in about six weeks. This is the fastest-paying single change available.

## EE. IELTS Speaking
### The format
| Part | Time | What happens |
| 1 | 4–5 min | ID check, then familiar topics: home, work, study, hobbies. 3 topic sets, ~4 questions each. |
| 2 | 3–4 min | Cue card. 1 minute to prepare, 1–2 minutes to speak alone, then 1–2 follow-up questions. |

### The four criteria — 25% each
| Criterion | What it measures |
| Fluency & Coherence | Can you keep going and stay logical? |
| Lexical Resource | Range, precision, paraphrase ability |
| Grammatical Range & Accuracy | Variety of structures + error rate |
| Pronunciation | ⭐ Intelligibility — including stress and rhythm |


⭐ Fluency does not mean speed. It means continuing without unnatural pauses. You can speak slowly and score Band 7. You cannot hesitate mid-sentence searching for grammar and score Band 7.

### Roughly what each band looks like
| Band | Description |
| 5 | Usually maintains flow but with repetition and self-correction; limited vocabulary; basic sentence forms; pronunciation causes some strain |
| 6 | Willing to speak at length, though coherence sometimes breaks; adequate vocabulary; mix of simple and complex forms with limited flexibility; generally understood, but mispronunciation reduces clarity at times |
| 7 | Speaks at length without noticeable effort; hesitation is content-related, not language-related; less common vocabulary with some awareness of style; range of complex structures; frequent error-free sentences |
| 8 | Fluent with only occasional repetition; wide vocabulary, skilful paraphrase; wide range of structures, majority error-free; easy to understand throughout |


### Part 1 strategy — the 3-sentence rule
Answer + Reason + Example. Not one word. Not a speech.
❌ Q: "Do you enjoy your job?" A: "Yes."
✅ "Yes, I do. I work as a geologist, so I spend a lot of time outdoors in the field, which suits me. Last summer I spent three months at a copper project in the South Gobi, and I really enjoyed it."

⚠️ You will be asked about your work. Rehearse a 30-second answer to "What do you do?" until it is completely automatic. It is the highest-probability question in the entire exam.
### Part 2 strategy
Use the full minute. Note keywords, never sentences — reading aloud from notes destroys your fluency score.
Cover all the bullet points
Keep talking until stopped. Stopping at 45 seconds caps your Fluency score.
If you run dry, expand: why it mattered · how you felt · what you'd do differently
### Part 3 strategy
Opinion → Reason → Example → Counterpoint

"I'd say technology has changed mining significantly. Automated drilling has improved safety, for instance, because fewer people work near active faces. That said, it has also reduced employment in some regions, so the benefits aren't evenly distributed."

That "That said" counterpoint is a Band 7 signal — it shows you can hold two ideas at once.
### Useful language
| Function | Phrases |
| Buying time | "That's an interesting question." · "Let me think for a moment." |
| Natural fillers | "well" · "actually" · "you know" · "I mean" |
| Opinion | "In my view…" · "As far as I'm concerned…" · "I'd say that…" |
| Hedging | "It depends, really." · "I suppose…" · "It tends to be…" |
| Extending | "for instance" · "which means that…" · "that's because…" |
| Self-correcting | "or rather" · "what I mean is…" |
| Counterpoint ⭐ | "That said…" · "Having said that…" · "On the other hand…" |


⚠️ Do not overuse time-buying phrases. One or two per test is natural; one before every answer is penalised as memorised filler.
### The five ways people lose marks
Memorised answers — heavily penalised and obvious to a trained ear
One-word answers in Part 1
Stopping early in Part 2
Short, unanalytical Part 3 answers
Forcing "impressive" words wrongly — Lexical Resource rewards appropriacy, not size

## GG. IELTS Reading
3 passages, 40 questions, 60 minutes, no extra transfer time.
### Question types, hardest last
| Type | Notes |
| Sentence / summary / table completion | Usually easiest — answers in order |
| Multiple choice | Moderate |
| Matching features / information | Answers not in order |
| Matching headings | Read the first and last sentence of each paragraph |
| TRUE / FALSE / NOT GIVEN | ⚠️ The hardest type in the exam |


### ⚠️ TRUE / FALSE / NOT GIVEN
| Answer | Means |
| TRUE | The passage states this |
| FALSE | The passage states the opposite |
| NOT GIVEN | The passage doesn't say |


The #1 error worldwide: choosing FALSE when the answer is NOT GIVEN.
FALSE = "the passage contradicts this" · NOT GIVEN = "the passage is silent on this"

⚠️ Use only the passage. Your own professional knowledge is irrelevant here and will actively mislead you.
### Time and method
20 minutes per passage. Passage 3 is hardest — some candidates do it first.
Do not read the whole passage first. Skim 1–2 minutes for structure, then work question by question.
Scan for synonyms. IELTS Reading is essentially a synonym-recognition test — the question never uses the passage's words.
Never leave blanks.
### 🔴 The honest diagnosis
Strategy will gain you about half a band. Vocabulary will gain you two.

IELTS Academic Reading needs roughly 7,000–8,000 word families to read comfortably. No technique compensates for words you don't know — you cannot recognise a synonym for a word you've never met.
If study time is limited, put it into vocabulary and reading volume, not practice tests. Practice tests measure the problem; they don't fix it.

| 6. Closing | "I look forward to hearing from you." |
| 7. Sign-off | "Kind regards, / Batbold" |


### Greetings and sign-offs — matched pairs
| Formality | Greeting | Sign-off |
| Very formal, name unknown | Dear Sir / Madam, | Yours faithfully, (UK) |
| Formal, name known | Dear Mr Baker, | Yours sincerely, (UK) |
| Standard business ⭐ | Dear John, | Kind regards, / Best regards, |
| Internal / familiar | Hi John, | Best, / Thanks, |


⚠️ "Dear Mr John" is wrong. Mr takes the surname only.
### Phrase bank
| Function | Phrases |
| Opening | "I hope this email finds you well." · "Thank you for your email of 12 May." · "Further to our conversation yesterday…" |
| Purpose | "I am writing to enquire about / request / confirm / follow up on…" |
| Requests ⭐ | "Could you please…" · "I would be grateful if you could…" · "I was wondering whether you might be able to…" |
| Attaching | "Please find attached…" · "I have attached…" |
| Bad news | "Unfortunately…" · "I regret to inform you that…" · "I apologise for any inconvenience." |
| Chasing | "I wanted to follow up on my email of 3 June." · "I don't seem to have received…" |
| Closing | "Please let me know if you have any questions." · "I look forward to hearing from you." · "Please do not hesitate to contact me." |


### ⚠️ Errors that damage professional image
| ❌ | ✅ | Why |
| "I am waiting your reply." | "I look forward to hearing from you." | Impatient and ungrammatical |
| "I look forward to hear…" | "I look forward to hearing…" | to is a preposition here (§O) |
| "Revert back to me." | "Get back to me." | Revert doesn't mean this internationally |
| "Please do the needful." | "Please take the necessary action." | Regional variety; unclear elsewhere |
| "Send me the data." | "Could you send me the data?" | Reads as an order (§1.4) |
| no subject line | always write one | Emails without subjects get ignored |


### A full worked example
Subject: Assay results — Khanbogd Phase 2 drilling
Dear Mr Baker,
I hope this email finds you well.
I am writing to update you on the Phase 2 drilling programme at Khanbogd. We have now completed all twelve planned holes, and samples were dispatched to ALS Ulaanbaatar on 3 June.
Preliminary results suggest that mineralisation continues at depth, although final assays are still pending. It should be noted that these results are provisional and subject to QAQC review.
Could you please confirm whether you would like a preliminary summary before the full report, which we expect to issue by 15 July?
Please do not hesitate to contact me if you have any questions.
Kind regards,
Batbold

Notice: passive voice, hedging (suggest, provisional), a polite modal request, and a clear action with a date.

### ⭐ Asking for repetition — the most important skill on this page
| Phrase | Use |
| "Sorry, could you repeat that?" | general |
| "I didn't quite catch that." | ⭐ natural and professional |
| "Sorry, I missed the last part." | precise — shows you followed most of it |
| "Would you mind speaking a little more slowly?" | polite, entirely acceptable |
| "Could you spell that for me?" | names, place names, chemicals |
| "Just so I'm clear — you're saying that…?" | ⭐ confirms and buys time |


Pretending to understand is the most expensive mistake a non-native professional makes. Asking is normal. Native speakers ask each other constantly. Nobody thinks less of an engineer who confirms a number.

Practise these until they're automatic. Under stress you'll only produce what's already procedural.

## JJ. Negotiation and diplomatic language
English carries politeness in grammar, not pronouns (§1.4). Negotiation is where that matters most.
### The seven softening techniques
| # | Technique | Direct ❌ | Diplomatic ✅ |
| 1 | Modal verbs | "That is impossible." | "That could be difficult." |
| 2 | Negative question | "We should reduce the scope." | "Wouldn't it be better to reduce the scope?" |
| 3 | Qualifiers | "The price is high." | "The price is slightly higher than expected." |
| 4 | Past / continuous for distance | "I want a discount." | "I was hoping we could discuss the price." |
| 5 | Restrictive openers | "No." | "I'm afraid that wouldn't work for us." |
| 6 | Impersonal ⭐ | "You made a mistake." | "There seems to be an error." |
| 7 | Positive framing | "That's wrong." | "That's not quite what we had in mind." |


### The translation table
| Direct | Diplomatic |
| I disagree. | I'm not sure I entirely agree. |
| That's impossible. | That might be difficult. |
| You're wrong. | I think there may be a misunderstanding. |
| No. | I'm afraid that wouldn't work for us. |
| We won't accept that. | That would be difficult for us to accept. |
| Your price is too high. | The price is somewhat higher than we'd anticipated. |
| You didn't send it. | I don't seem to have received it. ⭐ |
| You're late. | I understand there's been a delay. |


⭐ English blames the situation, not the person. "I don't seem to have received it" is grammatically about the speaker's inbox, not the listener's failure. This structural trick is everywhere in professional English and invisible to learners who translate directly.

### Negotiation phrases
| Stage | Language |
| Opening | "Our main priority is…" · "We're looking for…" |
| Conditional bargaining ⭐ | "If you could extend the timeline, then we would be able to reduce the day rate." |
| Conditions | "We'd be prepared to… provided that…" · "That depends on…" |
| Concession | "We could possibly consider…" · "There may be some flexibility on…" |
| Stalling | "I'll need to check with my colleagues." · "Let me take that away and come back to you." |
| Closing | "Shall we say 15 %?" · "Can we agree on that?" |


⭐ The first conditional (§S) is the core grammar of negotiation. "If you…, then we would…" is how every concession in English gets made. Grammar you learned as an exercise is a commercial tool.

## LL. The 12-month plan
### 🔴 Honest expectation setting
At 1 hour a day, twelve months gives roughly 330–365 hours. At the standard planning figure of ~200 hours per half band:
| Study time/day | Days to IELTS 7.5 from ~4.0 | ≈ Years |
| 1 hour | ~1,600 days | 4.5 |
| 2 hours | ~800 days | 2.2 |
| 3 hours | ~540 days | 1.5 |


Realistic 12-month target from IELTS 4.0–4.5: IELTS 5.5–6.0. Not 7.5.
Aim at 6.0, hit it, then decide whether the next campaign is worth it. A target you can reach keeps you studying; a target you can't makes you quit at month nine.

The variable you control is hours, not talent. Going from 1 to 2 hours a day halves the timeline.
### 📅 Q1 · Months 1–3 — Foundation
| Focus | Detail |
| Pronunciation | Word stress + connected speech, 15 min/day (§1.5, §DD) |
| Grammar | Errors 1–48 only: articles · word order · verb -s · plurals · copula · prepositions |
| Vocabulary | Reading + listening simultaneously; mine 5–8 words/day |
| Output | Write 100 words daily; get it corrected |


Checkpoint: ~4,500 words · graded readers easy · listening ~85%
### 📅 Q2 · Months 4–6 — Volume
| Focus | Detail |
| Reading | Move from graded readers to real books |
| Vocabulary | Latin/Greek roots (§G) — the biggest single unlock |
| Grammar | Present perfect · phrasal verbs · gerund vs infinitive |
| Speaking | ⭐ Start a tutor: 2 × 30 min/week. Non-negotiable — this is the correction loop. |


Checkpoint: ~5,500 words · first real novel finished · mock IELTS ≈ 5.0–5.5
### 📅 Q3 · Months 7–9 — Professional
| Focus | Detail |
| Register | Germanic/Latin pairs (§H) · academic hedging (§AA) |
| Writing | Business emails (§HH) · technical report language (§CC) |
| Speaking | Meeting language + asking for repetition (§II) |
| Grammar | Passive · relative clauses · conditionals |


Checkpoint: ~6,500 words · professional email unaided · mock ≈ 5.5–6.0
### 📅 Q4 · Months 10–12 — Exam and teaching
| Focus | Detail |
| IELTS | Full timed practice, all four papers |
| Reading | Timed passages — strategy on top of the vocabulary you now have |
| Speaking | Parts 1–3 under exam conditions |

Checkpoint: IELTS 5.5–6.0 · teaching beginners competently

### The four metrics that matter
| Metric | How | Frequency |

| Reading hours | log them | weekly |

### The five ways this plan fails
SRS avalanche — cap new cards at 15/day; suspend anything you keep failing
Skipping pronunciation because it feels slow — it's the highest-leverage work
Never getting corrected — input alone will not fix errors 1–100
Reading about English instead of using it — the fluency illusion
Aiming at 7.5 and quitting at month 9 — aim at 6.0

# Appendix — The core diagnosis
For a learner at roughly IELTS 4.0–4.5 who understands 60–80% of speech, finds reading hard, and can speak but not well:
| Symptom | Real cause |
| Reading is hardest | Vocabulary breadth. Conversation needs ~3,000 word families; novels and academic text need ~8,000–9,000. Sitting at ~3,500 makes speech manageable and text a wall. |
| Listening stuck at 60–80% | Mostly connected speech (§DD) — known words hidden inside reductions — plus the same vocabulary gap. |
| Speaking "not good" | Small chunk inventory (building word by word overloads working memory) + no correction loop, so errors have fossilised. |
| Weak sound–spelling link | Mongolian Cyrillic is phonetic; English spelling is a 500-year-old fossil (§L). Decoding is slow, which fills working memory and kills comprehension. |


### The three highest-leverage interventions
Read + listen simultaneously (same book as ebook and audiobook). Uses the strong channel (ears) to pull up the weak one (eyes), welds spelling to sound, and delivers reading and listening volume in one activity.
Connected speech dictation, 15 min/day for six weeks. The fastest measurable gain available.
A tutor with a shared error log. Uncorrected output myelinates errors. Two 30-minute sessions a week is the cheapest high-impact intervention that exists.
### Vocabulary coverage thresholds (Nation)
| Word families | What it gives you |
| 100 | ~50% of all running text |
| 1,000 | ~85% of spoken conversation |
| 3,000 | ~95% — reading possible with a dictionary |
| 5,000 | ~98% — reading becomes pleasant |
| 8,000–9,000 | wide reading of novels and news without support |


Reading is not one goal among four. It is the engine that powers all four. Words 3,000–9,000 live almost entirely in text — conversation will never teach them. Listening stays stuck near 80% until you have them.

End of guide.
